West Africa

From the Senegalese coast to the forests of Nigeria: a kitchen of rice, roots and red palm oil, with plenty of heat.

Food culture

Shared pots, big flavor

West Africa stretches from Senegal and Mali in the west to Nigeria and Cameroon in the east, and is home to hundreds of languages and food traditions. What links them is a love of one-pot cooking: rich stews and soups built on palm oil, tomatoes, onions and scotch bonnet peppers, eaten with a starch such as rice, fufu or gari.

Meals are often shared from a common bowl and guests are always offered food. Celebrations mean big pots of jollof, and street food is everywhere, from Nigerian suya grilled over charcoal to Ghanaian kelewele, plantain fried with ginger and chili.

Many deep flavors come from fermentation: dawadawa (also called iru or soumbala), made from fermented locust beans, gives soups a rich, savory taste.

Staples

What's in the pantry

Rice

African rice was first grown in the inner Niger Delta about 3,000 years ago.

Cassava

Turned into gari, fufu and attiéké; in some countries the leaves are cooked too.

Yam

Boiled, fried, or pounded into smooth, stretchy pounded yam.

Plantain

Fried as dodo or kelewele, or boiled and pounded with cassava.

Palm oil

Red, fragrant oil from the oil palm that colors and flavors stews.

Groundnuts

Peanuts thicken stews like mafé and flavor the yaji spice rub.

Cooking techniques

How West Africa cooks

  • Pounding: boiled yam, cassava or plantain is pounded in a large wooden mortar into smooth fufu.
  • Slow stewing: blended tomatoes, peppers and onions are fried in oil until thick and sweet, the start of jollof and many stews.
  • Fermenting: cassava becomes gari and attiéké, maize becomes ogi, and locust beans become dawadawa.
  • Charcoal grilling: thin strips of beef are rubbed with yaji, a peanut and chili spice mix, to make suya.
