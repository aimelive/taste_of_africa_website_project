North Africa

Between the Mediterranean and the Sahara: slow-cooked tagines, hand-rolled couscous and spice markets that have traded for centuries.

Food culture

Where trade routes meet

North Africa covers Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia and Libya, the region known as the Maghreb, with Egypt to the east. Its cooking grew from Amazigh (Berber) traditions, then absorbed Arab, Andalusian, Ottoman and French influences from everyone who passed through.

Food here balances sweet and savory: meat cooked with dried fruit, honey and nuts, warmed by spice blends like ras el hanout and the chili paste harissa. In 2020 UNESCO recognized the knowledge and traditions of couscous, nominated jointly by Algeria, Mauritania, Morocco and Tunisia.

Friday couscous with the family is a weekly ritual in many homes, and sweet mint tea, poured from high above the glass to make it foam, is offered to every guest.

Staples

What's in the pantry

Couscous

Semolina rolled by hand into tiny grains and steamed two or three times.

Bread

Round loaves like Moroccan khobz are used to scoop food at every meal.

Chickpeas & lentils

The base of soups like Moroccan harira and Egyptian koshari.

Olives & olive oil

Grown along the Mediterranean coast and used every day.

Preserved lemons

Lemons cured in salt for weeks; the soft rind adds a bright, salty kick.

Dates

Grown in desert oases and eaten to break the fast during Ramadan.

Cooking techniques

How North Africa cooks

  • Slow braising: meat and vegetables cook for hours in a clay tagine, whose cone lid returns steam to the pot.
  • Steaming: couscous sits in the top half of a couscoussier, steaming above a simmering stew.
  • Salt preserving: lemons, olives and smen (aged butter) are cured to keep for months.
  • Spice blending: ras el hanout can combine more than 20 spices, including cumin, cinnamon, ginger and saffron.
